## Spoken script

### 1. AI, innovation & digital business in Vietnam · 0:00–0:40

Good morning, and welcome. Thank you to UEH and SUSS for bringing us together. We are pleased to share our perspective as builders in Vietnam and learn from your experience across the region.

We will begin with Vietnam’s innovation context, then share our startup journey and the practical decisions behind it. Then we will discuss AI, innovation, and building a digital business. Finally, we will show our products as examples and invite your questions.

First, let us look at the wider context in Vietnam.

### 2. Vietnam’s innovation context · 0:40–1:35

This photo is from National Innovation Day last Sunday, 4 October. It brings Vietnam’s ambition to make innovation a driver of development into view. In its 5 October recap of National Innovation Day 2026, the National Innovation Center describes an effort to connect government, businesses, universities, experts, investors, and communities.

The activities range from embodied AI and clean hydrogen to STEM education and recognition for innovative products. Across these different fields, the common thread is collaboration and putting technology into practice.

For us, this raises a practical question: how can a small business put AI to work? Our connection to this direction is our interpretation, not a claim of government endorsement. With that context, let us turn to Mermail’s journey.

### 3. The Mermail journey · 1:35–2:35

Mermail’s product direction has evolved. In June, our initial focus was AI-assisted customer support: a hosted inbox where AI could prepare replies and a person would review them before sending.

By August, our direction had broadened to infrastructure for AI agents: a dedicated email identity, an inbox, and controlled payments as part of the product vision. These dates describe our product decisions, rather than the launch dates of every capability.

Today, we are bringing an application built on that foundation to market. We will show that application later.

The thread through this journey is email, useful actions, and human control. Here is how that shapes our product decisions.

### 4. Practical product decisions · 2:35–3:40

Three decisions connect our starting point to the direction we are taking today.

First, connect the steps. A draft is useful, but a business workflow also needs an identity, a place for messages, and a way to carry context into the next action. That pushed our thinking beyond generating text.

Second, keep human judgement in the process. AI can prepare research and messages. The person running the business decides whether the information is relevant and whether the proposed action is appropriate.

Third, choose a specific job. Finding customers involves research, contacts, outreach, and replies. For a small team, that preparation competes with running the business itself.

These are product decisions, not claims of measured customer results. Let us spend a moment on the human role.

### 5. Human judgement in AI workflows · 3:40–4:40

Human control is a practical design question. What information does a person need before trusting a proposed action? Where should they review it? How can they correct it?

A business knows things that a system may miss: an existing relationship, a sensitive account, an unsuitable offer, or a timing issue. Automating preparation does not remove that knowledge gap.

For outreach, review before sending is one useful boundary. Readable reasoning about why a company looks relevant is another, because a list of names alone is difficult to judge.

Our aim is useful assistance that people can supervise. The next question is how to turn that technical capability into a focused digital business.

### 6. Building a digital business · 4:40–5:45

Our approach combines a reusable foundation with a focused application. These serve different needs and make different kinds of value visible.

Infrastructure provides capabilities that applications can reuse: identity, communication, and permissions. A focused application combines relevant capabilities around a job that a business recognizes and wants to complete.

The application gives the foundation a practical setting. It helps us ask concrete questions about conversations, control, and reliability. The foundation, in turn, reduces the need to rebuild every underlying capability for each application.

This is our product strategy, not proof of commercial success. A digital business still has to establish that users value the workflow enough to adopt it. Today we are discussing that approach without claiming pricing or revenue results.

Now let us make these ideas concrete with our products.

### 7. Mermail: the infrastructure · 5:45–6:50

An AI agent needs a way to interact with the outside world. Mermail provides infrastructure around agent identity, email, and policy-controlled payments.

Identity gives the agent a recognizable presence. An inbox provides a place to send and receive messages and maintain a conversation. Payment controls are a separate capability for situations where an agent needs to act within defined spending permissions.

These are infrastructure capabilities, rather than a promise that every application uses every part. Our application, Scouty, focuses on customer development and outreach. We are not presenting payments as part of its demonstrated workflow.

The purpose of the infrastructure is to support useful applications with clear boundaries. Here is an illustrative journey showing how that idea applies to finding customers.

### 8. Scouty: finding relevant prospects · 6:50–8:20

Here is Scouty itself. This screenshot shows a prospect-discovery workflow using Mermail as the business being researched.

On the left, you can see the earlier steps: researching the company, exploring competitors, and defining campaigns. The selected campaign focuses on AI agent platforms. The main panel then brings together potential companies, descriptions, locations, company sizes, and AI-rated fit.

The important part is the context alongside each suggestion. A person can inspect what a company does and why it might be relevant before deciding whether to investigate further. The next step is to identify appropriate decision makers and prepare outreach for review.

These companies are prospect suggestions. Their appearance here does not mean they are our customers, have replied, or have agreed to a meeting. The fit scores are AI assessments to review, not guarantees.

This is one step in the broader journey from understanding a business to researching prospects, preparing relevant messages, and managing replies. It makes our infrastructure story tangible: helping a small team do connected, practical work.

That brings us to who we are taking Scouty to market for, and the problem we want to solve.

### 9. Bringing Scouty to market · 8:20–9:20

We are bringing Scouty to market for founders and small B2B teams. The pain point is straightforward: SMEs need new customers, but finding the right businesses and reaching the right people takes time and effort.

Before a conversation can start, someone has to research companies, identify decision makers, understand what might be relevant to them, and prepare an appropriate message. Small teams often have to do this alongside delivering their own product or service.

Scouty connects those steps: prospect discovery, personalized drafts, and reply management, with human review before sending. Our go-to-market focus is helping these teams spend less effort on preparation and more attention on customer conversations.

That is the business problem behind the application, and the next chapter of the Mermail journey.

## Q&A preparation

- **Is Scouty already producing quantified customer results?** We are bringing Scouty to market for SMEs and small B2B teams. We are not presenting verified performance figures today.
- **What is AutoGTM?** Our shorthand here is assistance with parts of go-to-market work: finding suitable companies and contacts, preparing outreach, and managing the next steps. It does not mean guaranteed sales or replacing the whole commercial team.
- **What is the relationship between Mermail and Scouty?** Mermail is the underlying agent infrastructure; Scouty is a focused application built on it. Not every Mermail capability is used in the illustrated Scouty flow.
- **Does Scouty make payments?** Payments are a separate Mermail infrastructure capability. This presentation does not claim that Scouty uses them.
- **Does the government support or fund this product?** We are describing our interpretation of alignment with Vietnam’s innovation direction. We are not claiming government endorsement, funding, or a formal partnership.
- **Can outreach run without human review?** The workflow presented today emphasizes review before sending and user control. Discuss only controls confirmed in the product; do not promise unverified policy or compliance features.
- **Where does lead information come from, and how do you handle privacy?** The public product describes prospect and company research. Do not improvise a vendor list, data provenance guarantee, certification, or legal conclusion. Offer to follow up with the exact product documentation for a specific use case.
- **How will you enter other ASEAN markets?** Regional relevance is a hypothesis to explore. Local language, buying behaviour, data quality, and trust need validation; this deck does not announce a launch schedule.
- **What does it cost?** Pricing is outside this presentation. Refer to current product information or a follow-up conversation rather than stating unconfirmed figures.
